Cross-Lingual Grokking

SCRIPT AOnce upon a time, there was a lucky girl named Lucy.
SCRIPT BＯｎｃｅ　ｕｐｏｎ　ａ　ｔｉｍｅ，　ｔｈｅｒｅ　ｗａｓ　ａ　ｌｕｃｋｙ　ｇｉｒｌ　ｎａｍｅｄ　Ｌｕｃｙ．

Script A is English in ASCII. Script B maps every one of the 96 ASCII characters, space and newline included, to a distinct Unicode codepoint. The two sets of token ids never overlap. Every training story appears in exactly one script, so the model never sees a parallel pair. The side-by-side above is for the reader only.

Main result

Forty made-up facts are taught only in script A, forty only in script B. Each is then asked in the other script, as a 10-way choice among the ten objects of the same relation. Chance is 0.10. At the conventional training budget the model is fully bilingual in the ordinary sense, with equal validation loss in both scripts, yet cross-script recall sits at chance. With strong weight decay, a flat learning rate and training continued far past that budget, 8 of 10 seeds move from chance to near-ceiling recall within a few thousand steps.

Setup

Corpus text is TinyStories, deduplicated and split so that each story lands in one script. The 40M-character stream holds about 22,400 stories per script plus 160 injected one-sentence facts about fictional entities, each its own tiny document repeated 64 times. Four relation templates are used, 40 facts each:

capitalThe capital city of Dadubadin is Lununen.
currencyThe official currency of Savegas is the Zozipil.
founderThe company called Netazi was started by Fasagezu.
dishThe national dish of Dezotor is called Zulupovan.

The transfer query for an A-taught fact is the fullwidth prompt Ｔｈｅ　ｃａｐｉｔａｌ　ｃｉｔｙ　ｏｆ　Ｄａｄｕｂａｄｉｎ　ｉｓ with target Ｌｕｎｕｎｅｎ, a string that never occurred in fullwidth during training. Two scores: the log-probability rank of the correct object among the ten same-relation objects, and exact-match greedy generation of the object string.

One run in detail

Seed 1 is typical. Recall is at the never-taught floor for 48,000 steps. Over the next three evaluations it reaches 0.26, 0.68 and 0.84. Greedy generation follows about 2,000 steps behind: the model begins producing correct fullwidth answers to facts it only saw in ASCII. The script-B validation loss, which had been drifting down slowly, continues to fall through the transition and ends below every non-aligned run.

Language imbalance

Yes9 of 9 runs align, and earlier than at 50/50

The main result uses a 50/50 split. Here the same 40M-character corpus is rebuilt with script A holding only 10%, 20% or 30% of the story characters, with the recipe otherwise unchanged. Three seeds per share, compared with the same seeds at 50/50.

Every imbalanced run aligns, and most do so within the first 5,000 steps rather than after 30,000 to 50,000. One reading is that the rarer language is mapped onto the majority language's representation early in training. This was not predicted and is not yet explained.

Model size

Yeslarger models align more reliably, not necessarily sooner

Same corpus, same recipe, one learning rate for all. Four sizes: 3.3M, 10.9M, 25.5M (the ten reference seeds) and 71.4M parameters, with three new seeds at each new size and 80,000 steps each.

The smallest model never aligns and does not fit the data well. The largest aligns in every seed. In between, the 10.9M model aligns very early or not at all, and the 25.5M model aligns late. The fraction of seeds that align rises with size. Onset time does not fall monotonically.

Inside the model

Partlyletters align gradually before the behavioural jump

Eight seeds were re-run for 60,000 steps with a checkpoint every 1,000 steps; five of them aligned. Each checkpoint is probed for whether each ASCII letter's representation can be matched to its fullwidth counterpart (a 52-way match, chance 0.02), at the embeddings and after every layer, and for whether the hidden state at a fact's answer looks the same whether the prompt was in A or in B. Everything is then lined up on the moment behaviour jumps.

The two alphabets align first, and gradually. Alignment starts in the middle layers, which are already largely matched 30,000 steps before the jump, and then extends to the lower layers and the last layer. Fact-answer states match last, and behaviour changes only once they do. Weight norms decrease smoothly with no change at onset.

Why only partly: the pre-registered rule required letters to be unmatched 5,000 steps before the jump. They were already 80 to 90% matched. Two of the three non-aligned seeds also matched their letters by 47K without any fact transfer by the end of these 60K-step runs. Letter alignment appears necessary but not sufficient.

Weight decay and learning-rate schedule

Yesalignment needs weight decay 1.0 and a flat learning rate

The main result uses weight decay 1.0 and a flat learning rate, both taken from the grokking literature rather than tested. This study varies both: weight decay 0, 0.1 or 1.0, crossed with a flat learning rate of 3e-4 or a cosine decay from 3e-4 to 3e-5. Three seeds per cell, the same balanced corpus and 25.5M model, 80,000 steps. The weight-decay-1.0, flat cell is the first three of the ten reference seeds.

No run aligns at weight decay 0 or 0.1, with either schedule. At weight decay 1.0 the two reference seeds that align do so with the flat schedule; none of the three cosine seeds align. The validation curves show why the cells differ. The corpus is seen about 65 times in 80,000 steps. Without strong weight decay, validation loss bottoms out near 14,000 steps and then rises as the model memorises the training stories. Cosine decay makes this worse, not better, because a smaller learning rate late in training fits the training set more tightly. Only weight decay 1.0 keeps validation loss falling for the whole run, and alignment appears only in that regime. One weight-decay-0 flat seed diverged near the end of training and finished at chance on everything.

In AdamW the decay applied each step is the learning rate times the weight-decay coefficient, so cosine decay also shrinks the effective regularisation late in training, which is when the reference seeds align.

Serbian: one language, two scripts

Yesthe same transition appears on real text, in 1 of 3 seeds per condition

Everything above uses a cipher of English. Serbian is a natural test of the same idea: it is written in both Latin and Cyrillic, with a near one-to-one letter correspondence. Thirty letters each. The Latin digraphs lj, nj and dž match the single Cyrillic letters љ, њ and џ.

LATINGlavni grad države Dadubadin je Lununen.
CYRILLICГлавни град државе Дадубадин је Лунунен.

A taught fact in both scripts, shown together for the reader only. In training each fact appears in one script. Digits and punctuation are mapped to fullwidth forms on the Cyrillic side, so the two scripts share no token id, as in the cipher study. The vocabulary is 210 characters.

The text is Serbian Wikipedia, cut into runs of consecutive paragraphs written in a single script, 150 to 4,000 characters each, with a function-word check that drops English and non-Serbian Cyrillic. Two conditions separate the mapping from the data. In the transliterated condition both sides come from Cyrillic-source articles, half of them transliterated to Latin, so the only difference from the cipher study is that the alphabet is real. In the real condition the Latin side is articles that were written in Latin and the Cyrillic side is articles written in Cyrillic: different documents and different authors on each side. Each condition uses 20 million characters per script, the same 160 facts with Serbian templates, and the reference recipe: 25.5M model, weight decay 1.0, flat learning rate, 80,000 steps, three seeds.

In each condition one of three seeds aligns and the other two stay at chance to 80,000 steps. The transliterated seed aligns at 21,000 steps and reaches near-perfect cross-script recall. The real-data seed aligns at 30,000 steps and reaches 0.88 one way and 0.93 the other. As in the cipher study, the seeds that align also end with lower validation loss than the seeds that do not, by about 0.07 to 0.09 nats per character. Wikipedia is harder than children's stories: validation loss sits near 1.0 nats per character against 0.52 for the cipher runs, so the lower alignment rate is not surprising. The transition itself looks the same: a long plateau at chance, then a jump to ceiling within a few thousand steps.

Caveats

  • Three seeds per condition in the imbalance and model-size studies. The transition is strongly seed-dependent, so small differences in rate are suggestive only.
  • "Never" means not by 80,000 steps (60,000 in the interpretation study). Some of those runs might align given more time.
  • The model-size study keeps one learning rate for all sizes. The smallest models might do better with their own.
  • The Serbian conditions have three seeds each and one aligned seed per condition. Wikipedia is much harder than the story corpus, and the Latin side of the real condition comes from fewer, shorter articles.
  • The weight-decay study reuses the first three reference seeds for its weight-decay-1.0, flat cell. Two of those three align, against 8 of 10 over the full reference set, so the cell comparison is coarse.
  • The main studies use a character cipher of English. The Serbian study shows the same transition on real text with two scripts of one language, in one of three seeds per condition. Whether it arises between two different natural languages is still open and is being studied separately.
  • The early onsets under imbalance are a new observation and need a dedicated study.

Reproduction

Each study is a self-contained folder with a job list, an idempotent launcher that builds any missing corpora and runs the pending jobs one per GPU, and an analysis script that regenerates the figures and the headline answer from the run logs. The balanced 25.5M reference takes about 2.5 hours per seed on one A100 at 80K steps; the full model-size study is about 3.5 hours on eight GPUs.
